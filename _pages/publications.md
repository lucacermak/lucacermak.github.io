My research studies how behavioral biases shape decisions with high stakes, using natural experiments in aviation, professional sports and household finance, and online experiments.

## Job Market Paper

### Stated Fear and Revealed Avoidance: Evidence from the Boeing 737 MAX Crashes
*Single authored*

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Abstract</summary>
The Boeing 737 MAX crashed twice within five months but was grounded only after the second. In monthly data on all US airline-routes since 2016, an airline-route flown entirely by the MAX filled ten percent fewer of its seats after the first crash, and on domestic routes fares fell as well, most clearly at Southwest. Travelers avoided the airline-route rather than the MAX flight. Their avoidance did not fade as public attention to the crash faded, and most of it ended with the grounding. Combining the fall in fares and in passengers, the price of trust is about thirty dollars per ticket. This implies a perceived fatality risk far above the regulator's estimate and about three quarters of the frequency of one crash in the MAX's flights. Surveys after the second crash recorded up to three times as much stated avoidance.
</details>

## Working Papers

### [Action Bias and the Size of the Action Space: Evidence from a Natural Experiment in Football](https://ssrn.com/abstract=5388421)
*Single authored, under review*

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Abstract</summary>
What does a decision maker under pressure do with a larger action space? I exploit a natural experiment in professional football, where a policy change increased the substitution allowance from three to five. The English Premier League then returned to three for two seasons before readopting five, while four other leagues kept five throughout. Using minute-level data on 7,203 matches and 55,963 substitutions, I separate two decisions, whether to intervene and what strategy the intervention carries. I show that the policy led to more substitutions when teams underperformed expectations, and that the tactical adjustment each one carried was smaller. This effect is concentrated among strong favourites. Substitutions rose across all types, including strategically neutral and defensive ones, which rejects rational risk-taking and is consistent with action bias. These results challenge the classical assumption that a decision maker will disregard the options a larger action space adds.
</details>

### Resilient Workers, Biased Evaluations: Disentangling Performance from Assessment
*With Alexander Rieber and Dennis Steinle, under review*

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Abstract</summary>
Discrimination in performance evaluations is hard to measure because evaluators' judgments are rarely compared with an algorithmic benchmark of the same performance. We exploit such a benchmark in European football. Using 355,000 algorithmically rated player-matches across ten leagues, 67,000 of which are also rated by professional sports journalists, we find that minority players, defined by origin and skin tone, receive journalist ratings 0.05 to 0.06 standard deviations lower than majority players with the same algorithmic scores. We use COVID-19 stadium closures to test whether crowd pressure influences players or journalists, but find no systematic change in performance or the rating gap.
</details>
